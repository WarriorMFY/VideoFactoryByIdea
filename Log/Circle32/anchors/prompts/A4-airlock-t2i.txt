Interior of a NASApunk launch complex: close view of the airlock door at the end of the white ceramic-paneled corridor. Camera about 3.5 meters in front of the door, lens at 1.2 meters height, tilted slightly upward, so the door fills the central 55 percent of the frame and its vertical edges converge gently toward the top.

The airlock is a two-leaf sliding pressure door: white ceramic panels, a round porthole at the center with a thick metal rim, the stenciled word AIRLOCK above the porthole, orange-and-black hazard chevrons across the lower part. The two leaves are caught mid-travel, closed to within a 20 cm gap, the seam slightly off-center, a thin line of cool white light leaking through. A recessed steel track runs across the top of the frame.

On the right side of the door frame, at head height, sits a single amber rotating beacon inside a protective metal cage, unlit for now. Heavy metal locking lugs sit at the four corners of the frame, and a band of orange-and-white hazard striping runs along the top lintel.

Flanking walls: white ceramic panels with visible seams and rivets, neatly bundled orange cable harnesses held by chrome clamps, one small grey electrical box. On the floor, yellow-black safety lines run straight toward the door over steel grating. Overhead: exposed tidy piping and a single industrial work lamp.

Lighting: high-key cool white overhead light, a warm amber practical glow from the beacon housing, a thin warm spill from the door gap. Materials matte and clean, optimistic 1970s space-program engineering. No people, no text other than AIRLOCK, no neon, no rust.
